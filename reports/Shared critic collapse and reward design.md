# A rare crash penalty hijacks shared critics

The shared 4-head critic fails because one trunk has to serve two kinds of group at once. The safety group's rare 400-point crash dominates every shared gradient. The other three groups are made only of per-step costs, so each of them gains when the episode ends early. Our own Monte Carlo check shows that the collapse is an estimation error, not real structure. The true per-group discounted returns along stored trajectories correlate only −0.19 to +0.19, while the shared critic's heads on the same states correlate 0.89 to 0.99. Its second layer has 39 % dead FAST_TANH units and a feature srank of 11/64. Bootstrapped TD theory predicts this outcome: heads that read one shared, TD-trained representation drift toward multiples of the same dominant direction. The multi-task RL literature documents the scale-driven dominance that picks that direction. Every rebalancing we tried failed for a reason the reward itself explains. Without the total-value loss term, crashes rise to 39 % (shared) and 41.5 % (split). That term is the only channel that carries crash value into the three cost heads, and normalisation or scale-invariant aggregation cuts the remaining counterweight. The literature has close precedents: TOPPO's co-linear critic gradients, MultiCriticAL's result that separate critics beat a multi-head critic, SAC-D's termination-driven conflicts, and Bootstrapped DQN's 1/K trunk scaling. None of them reports value-level collapse of separately supervised reward heads in an off-policy TD critic, its effect on actor-side aggregators, or per-objective termination bias. The fixes most likely to work therefore change two things: what reaches the shared trunk, and how the crash is priced across groups. Examples are splitting the crash penalty across groups, giving safety its own network, or turning the crash into a crash-probability constraint. More normalisation or critic-side aggregation is not one of them.

## The collapse is a bootstrapped estimation error, not shared structure

A reviewer's first objection would be that the group values ought to correlate. Under a fixed policy, every group value is Q_k = (I − γP^π)^{-1} r_k. All four heads share the same resolvent, so weakly correlated rewards can in principle produce strongly correlated values. Our Monte Carlo check at seed 70 and 2M steps rules this out. **The true per-group discounted returns correlate −0.19 to +0.19 on the shared-critic run and −0.06 to +0.22 on the split run. On the same states, the critics' group values correlate 0.89 to 0.99 (shared) and 0.48 to 0.72 (split)** (our measurement). The shared critic's near-copies are therefore an error in the estimate. The split critic is also over-correlated, by roughly 0.4 to 0.5, a point taken up in the next section. This is the reference level the earlier notes recommended before any collapse claim. It turns "values are 0.9+ correlated" into "values exceed the true return correlation by about 0.8".

Two published mechanisms combine to explain the error. The first is the implicit under-parameterisation result of Kumar et al. (ICLR 2021): **bootstrapped TD training lowers the effective rank of a Q-network's penultimate features, while supervised regression to fixed targets "does not cause rank collapse"**. In their kernel model, the learned value is Q_k = M_k R, and the singular-value ratios of M_k shrink with each fitting iteration ([Kumar et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14498)). With a shared trunk, every head is the same increasingly low-rank operator applied to its own reward. As M_k approaches σ₁u₁v₁ᵀ, every head's value tends to a multiple of the same vector u₁, whatever the reward correlation. This extrapolation to multiple heads is ours, not the paper's. Lyle et al. (AISTATS 2021) give the matching result for several value functions. **TD learning pulls their deviations toward the top eigenfunctions of the shared transition operator, even with zero reward, while Monte Carlo learning "does not pick up any additional information about the environment"** ([Lyle et al. 2021](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2102.13089)). Both predict four things we observe: the collapse needs bootstrapping, the TD targets inherit it, extra width does not cure it (Kumar: "even high-capacity value networks behave as low-capacity networks"), and separate critics, which each have their own kernel, escape it.

The second mechanism decides which direction u₁ the heads collapse onto. Multi-task PopArt found that "the saliency of a task for the agent increases with the scale of the returns observed in that task" ([Hessel et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1809.04474)). **TOPPO measured per-task critic gradient norms about 497x apart in a shared multi-task critic**, close to our 100-3000x safety-to-time ratio ([Li et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.11473v3)). PaCo describes our H4 almost word for word: an oversized task loss "will contribute a significant portion in updating the shared parameters because of their dominance" and "significantly impact the training of the other tasks" ([Sun et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.11653)). SAC-D found the same effect for a rare termination. In Ant, "these rare instances in the replay buffer were associated with very large critic losses" and caused "catastrophic gradient conflicts" in its shared multi-head critic ([MacGlashan et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2206.13901)). Lyle et al. (2024) add a dose-response result: **a large regression-target magnitude alone reduces a network's ability to fit other targets, and LayerNorm reduces but does not remove the effect** ([Lyle et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18762)). Safety's TD-target spread of 152, against 3.2 for time, is exactly that kind of offset.

Our saturation measurements show what this does to the hard-clipped FAST_TANH trunk. FAST_TANH passes exactly zero gradient at |z| ≥ 3. **At 2M steps, 41 % of the shared critic's layer-2 pre-activations were saturated and 39 % of its units were dead (saturated on at least 99 % of states), against 29 % and 18 % in the split critic** (our measurement). Dead units are a recognised plasticity pathology for tanh networks: a dead unit can be "a tanh [with] very large preactivation values that take it into its 'constant' region" ([Lyle et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18762)). The dormancy score used by ReDo cannot see them, because a saturated unit has |h| = 1 and scores as highly active ([Sokar et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12902)). Low rank alone is not the failure. The split networks also have low feature srank (38, 5, 13 and 3), and rank and performance are known to be only loosely linked ([Gulcehre et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02099)). The difference is that the split critic gives each group its own basis, about 59 directions in total, while **the shared critic makes all four heads read one 11-dimensional basis**. Two further facts argue against slow plasticity loss as the main cause. First, the head-scaled critic collapses within about 18k critic updates (correlation 1.00 at 200k steps). Second, our update ratio of 0.1 critic updates per environment step is ten times below the regimes where resets and ReDo matter, and the split critic runs on the same schedule ([Nikishin et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.07802); [Sokar et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12902)). What remains is optimisation geometry: a dominant head saturating a shared trunk whose operator is already being made low-rank by bootstrapping.

## Three of four heads correctly prefer a crash

The deeper problem sits in the reward decomposition. Kostrikov et al. showed that **a strictly negative reward "penalizes every step and leads to collapsing in environments with a survival bonus"**, and that most methods implicitly give absorbing states zero value ([Kostrikov et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1809.02925)). L2F adds its survival constant explicitly "to mitigate the 'learning to terminate' problem" ([Eschmann et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13081)). legged_gym clips negative totals at zero to avoid "early termination problems" ([legged_gym config](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/leggedrobotics/legged_gym/master/legged_gym/envs/base/legged_robot_config.py)). Applied per objective, the consequence is direct. Time, precision, efficiency, and the dense orientation and landing terms inside safety are all per-step costs. A crash ends every one of these streams, and the −400 sits only in the safety group. So **for every cost group, Q_k(s, crash-bound action) ≥ Q_k(s, safe action). Wherever an action can change the crash probability, three of the four actor gradients point toward crashing, and only the safety head points away**. In variant E's scalar reward this cancels inside the sum. Once the groups are learned and aggregated separately, the cancellation holds only if safety's slope arrives at the actor intact and large enough. We found no paper that analyses termination bias per objective in a decomposed critic.

Our total-value-loss ablation is the clearest evidence for this view. **Without the (Σₖ eₖ)² term, the plain shared critic crashes in 39 % of test episodes (circle 18.5 cm) and the split critic in 41.5 % (circle 12.4 cm). With the term, crashes fall to 4.5 % and 2.4 %** (our measurement, 2 seeds). The gradient of that term with respect to every head's output is 2Σₖeₖ. On crash transitions, the crash error is therefore injected into all four heads, so each cost head partly learns the crash value it would otherwise ignore. Two consequences follow, and both are our inferences. First, the working split critic is not four independent networks: the total term couples them, and that coupling is what makes it safe. This should be stated plainly in the paper, before a reviewer finds it. Second, the same term pushes a shared error component into every head, which plausibly explains why even the split critic's values (0.48 to 0.72) correlate well above the true returns (−0.06 to +0.22). It also fits the offline finding that PGA aggregators with the total term wrecked the small time and precision heads (R² −4.5 and −2.8): the total error dominates heads whose own spread is tiny.

This framing explains why every normalisation failed. A std or RMS scale for a group that contains the crash is set by the crash outliers: our one-shot scale for safety came out at 6389, inflated by early crashes. Dividing by it removes exactly the signal the cost heads need to be outweighed. PopArt equalises task influence by design, so that "all tasks have a similar impact on the learning dynamics" ([Hessel et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1809.04474)). That is correct when tasks are equally important. It is wrong when the actor's utility is the real-unit sum and three heads want the episode to end. Liu et al. saw the same pattern in a routed multi-head critic: adding PopArt "reduces the aggregate normalized training gain on the pinned levels from +24.2% to +10.0%" ([Liu et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.26481v1)). Our actor logs show the mechanism directly. **In normalised critics that did not collapse but still crashed, the actor aggregator gave safety a weight of 0.13 to 0.23, against about 0.5 with the split critic, while the safety and efficiency action-slopes were 2 to 4 times steeper than the split critic's** (our measurement). Scale-invariant aggregation down-weights a steep gradient, so a sharper safety slope bought less safety weight, and the steeper efficiency slope pushed harder in the direction the termination bias already favours. **In the collapsed shared critic, OS-NMF-PGA put weights of 21 to 35 on time and negative weights on the other three groups, with a mean gradient cosine of +0.72 to +0.81** (our measurement). A unit-norm principal direction expressed in near-parallel gradients gives the smallest gradient the largest weight and subtracts the residuals of the others, including safety's. SAC-D reported the milder version of this for CAGrad inside a critic: "the higher the critic loss, the lower the weight CAGrad tended to assign the component" ([MacGlashan et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2206.13901)).

## Accurate values say little about the slopes the actor uses

The repeated observation that critics with R² near 0.99 still give policies that crash 18-95 % of the time follows from deterministic policy-gradient theory. Silver et al.'s compatibility conditions are stated entirely in terms of ∇ₐQ at the policy's action. The state-value part of the critic is arbitrary, and with an approximate critic the update "will not necessarily follow the true gradient (nor indeed will it necessarily be an ascent direction at all)" ([Silver et al. 2014](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v32/silver14.pdf)). MAGE turns this into a bound: **the policy-gradient error is at most L_π/(1−γ) times the expected error of the critic's action-gradient, and the value error does not appear at all** ([D'Oro & Jaśkowski 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.14309)). On Pendulum, a TD-trained critic "completely fails" to predict the true action-gradient, which undermines the "common assumption" that minimising TD error also fixes the gradients ([D'Oro & Jaśkowski 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.14309)). The on-policy analogue is Ilyas et al.'s value network, which fits its loss and generalises yet is "off by about 50%" from the true value ([Ilyas et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1811.02553)). The Bellman error is itself a poor proxy for value accuracy ([Fujimoto et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.12417)).

Two consequences are specific to our setup. First, R² is nearly blind to slopes here. Late in training the exploration std has decayed to about 0.02, so the action-dependent share of each head's target variance is about σₐ²·E‖∇ₐQ_k‖². That share stays below the 1 % residual allowed by R² = 0.99 unless the slope exceeds about 5 target-stds per unit action, roughly 760 for safety. This is our arithmetic, but it means that a head can lose its action-dependence entirely without R² noticing. Second, the per-head version of MAGE's bound shows why the sum aggregator is robust and the others are not. **Sum needs only the summed slope error to be small, so per-head errors that cancel do no harm. UPGrad, CAGrad, PCGrad and the PGA family need every head's gradient to be accurate, and the scale-invariant ones need every head's direction.** The small time and precision heads, which PGA weights most, have the least accurate directions because the trunk is fitted to safety. A compatibility result for vector-valued critics or non-linear aggregators does not exist in the literature we searched; the extension is ours.

A shared trunk also restricts which slopes the heads can express. With trunk features h(s,a) and linear heads, every head's slope is ∇ₐQ_k = M(s,a)w_k. Here M is built from the action columns of the first layer and the FAST_TANH derivatives, which are zero for saturated units. All four slope fields are therefore combinations of the same basis fields, shaped by the dominant loss, while head values can still differ through state-only features. This is how the PopArt-like critic could have distinct values (−0.98 to +0.93) and R² of 0.97 to 0.99 and still crash. Our twin-agreement measurement narrows which form of H2 is at work. **The twins' action-slopes agree at cosine 0.71 to 0.86 (shared) against 0.71 to 0.92 (split)** (our measurement), so slope *variance* hardly differs between the two architectures. Both twins regress onto the same targets, so their agreement says nothing about bias. Whether the shared critic's slopes are systematically wrong needs Monte Carlo finite-difference slopes per group, and that test has not been run.

The literature offers three families of slope-aware fixes. One avoids the point slope altogether: Compatible Policy Gradient replaces ∇ₐQ with a two-point Gaussian-smoothing estimate at μ = 0.1. That needs correct values within ±μ of the policy's action rather than correct derivatives, and it improved TD3 on Ant (4225 to 5285) and HalfCheetah (6745 to 7957) ([Saglam & Kalogerias](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.01477)). A second changes the architecture: a hypernetwork critic in which the state generates the weights of an action network "enjoys better gradient accuracy" in TD3 and SAC ([Keynan et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06842)). An advantage decomposition Q = V(s) + A(s,a) − A(s,π(s)), as in Deep Advantage Updating, moves the large state-driven variance out of the part the actor differentiates ([Tallec et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1901.09732)). The third supervises the slope with a model, as MAGE does. It needs a differentiable dynamics model and degenerates to ∇ₐQ ≈ 0 without a value term. Two popular regularisers are poor choices. In PAVE's TD3 appendix, critic spectral normalisation "collapsed the policy", and a plain λ‖∇ₐQ‖² penalty never beat the baseline and collapsed Walker at λ = 10 ([Lee et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.22970)). Our schedule also strips away TD3's built-in action smoothing late in training. Target noise decays to about 0.04 by 2M steps, whereas TD3's ablation shows that target smoothing helps on three of four tasks ([Fujimoto et al. 2018](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1802.09477)).

A forward-citation sweep covered 302 papers citing HRA, 13-14 citing SAC-D and 12 citing GCR-PPO, together with 18 arXiv abstract queries. None of them reports value-head collapse in a decomposed critic. Several precedents do narrow the claim, and they must be cited. The table separates what is published from what was not found.

Three corrections to the earlier novelty review apply. The 2.3x summed-head step and its 1/K fix are not novel: Bootstrapped DQN gives the same reason ("effectively increases the learning rate for this portion of the network"), and SAC-D uses the same scaling. "Shared Actors Need Not Share Critics" is now verified as arXiv 2608.26481. It concerns a scalar critic shared across environments, and its key line is "the practical tradeoff is therefore between statistical sharing and separation of conflicting value targets" ([Liu et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.26481v1)). GCR-PPO's critic is a fully shared trunk with an unweighted summed MSE, verified from its code ([GCR-PPO actor_critic.py](https://github.com/humphreymunn/GCR-PPO/blob/HEAD/rsl_rl/rsl_rl/modules/actor_critic.py)). Its low quadcopter conflict (0.15) is therefore ambiguous: a partly collapsed shared critic would also report low conflict.

The strongest counter-example to "shared fails" is Kim et al. (T-RO 2024). There, a dense 10-head cost critic in PPO matched separate critics with about 10x fewer parameters (prediction loss 0.468 against 0.435), and the authors concluded that "sharing the neural network backbone is permissible" ([Kim et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.12517v4)). Their setting differs from ours in exactly the places our analysis says matter. They use GAE targets, which are closer to Monte Carlo than 1-step TD. Each cost advantage is standardised. No 400-point terminal penalty sits in one head. And the actor learns from advantages, not from ∇ₐQ.

A safe wording, refined from the novelty note with our Monte Carlo result, reads as follows. "In a reward-decomposed TD3 critic whose heads share one trunk, the per-objective heads converge to near-copies of each other, even though every head is trained on every transition with its own reward and its own bootstrapped TD target. Their pairwise correlation reaches 0.89–1.00, against −0.19 to +0.19 for the true per-group Monte Carlo returns on the same states, and the TD targets inherit the same correlation. Because the actor's per-objective gradients pass through these heads, they become near-parallel, and every multi-objective gradient aggregator we tested reduces to the plain sum. A wider shared critic with the same parameter count as the separate critics still collapses. To our knowledge, neither this value-level collapse nor its effect on actor-side aggregation has been reported. It differs from the co-linearity of per-task critic-loss gradients in multi-task PPO (Li et al., 2026) and from the performance advantage of separate over multi-headed critics in task-routed multi-style RL (Mysore et al., 2022)." Add the hedge "in the literature we surveyed up to 27 November 2026". Avoid the terms "head collapse", "co-linear collapse", "value function collapse" and "value function interference". All four are already used elsewhere, in other senses ([Vamplew et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.06266v2)). "Value-head collapse in reward-decomposed critics" is free. Before submission, three papers that the sweep could not open still need reading: Fan et al.'s SAC with a multi-head critic for quadruped locomotion, Applied Intelligence 2025 ([dblp](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/journals/apin/FanPSLGT25.html)); the IROS 2026 role-based reward decomposition study, which compares single-critic and multi-critic designs and reports "value interference" ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/IROS61738.2026.11776619)); and a 2026 batch-process safe-RL paper with multi-head critics. TOPPO's off-policy MTSAC appendix also remains unread.

## Partial sharing must keep safety's gradient out of the other heads

Supervised multi-task learning offers many partial-sharing designs, and they differ in one property that matters here: whether the dominant group's gradient can reach another group's parameters. In MMoE every expert is shared, so the 100-3000x safety gradient trains every expert. PLE adds task-specific experts explicitly to escape the "seesaw phenomenon" in which improving one task lowers others ([Tang et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1145/3383313.3412236)). Recon finds that gradient surgery "cannot effectively reduce the occurrence of conflicting gradients", while turning the highest-conflict shared layers into task-specific layers does ([Shi et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.11289)). In multi-task RL, task-specific structure in the critic matters: PaCo scores 85.4 with composition in both actor and critic, against 73.3 in the actor only ([Sun et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.11653)). Capacity alone did not close the gap: a 4.2x-wider multi-head SAC reached 50.3 % on MT50, against 60.0 % for Soft Modularization ([Yang et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.13661)). No paper tests any of these designs in a decomposed critic, so the ranking below is our extrapolation. Parameter counts are for one twin.

| Design | Params | Mechanism and risk |
|---|---|---|
| Safety-split hybrid: safety gets its own 64-64 network; time, precision and efficiency share a 64-64 trunk with 3 heads | 13,188 (0.50x split) | Removes the 400-point targets and the 100-3000x gradient from the shared trunk, and keeps safety's value and slope as in the working split critic. The three remaining groups differ by only about 5x in spread. Risk: efficiency stopped fitting in the towers critic. Our earlier towers design shared layer 1 with safety, so this design is untested. |
| Narrow split, 4 × 32-32, no sharing | 8,964 (1.33x shared) | A mandatory control. If it matches the 64-wide split critic, the reviewer's "4 networks" concern disappears without any sharing. |
| Shared-private (PLE/CGC style): shared 64-64 plus a private 16-16 or 32-32 expert per group | 10,180-15,684 | Gives each group a private (s,a) feature and slope basis. Risk: heads lean on the safety-shaped shared features and collapse again. |
| Cross-stitch with narrow columns, mixing initialised at identity | 8,996 | Starts as a narrow split critic. Learned mixing lets safety's gradient leak in. |
| MMoE, FiLM, LoRA, task embedding | 6,785-14,244 | Every parameter stays exposed to safety, so these are expected to collapse like the shared trunk. A naive Mix-Expert baseline reached 42.8 % against 85.0 % for multi-head SAC ([Yang et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.13661)). |

Loss balancing inside the critic should follow one rule: never rebalance a shared part that contains safety. Most adaptive balancers are size-blind by construction. At equilibrium, Kendall-style uncertainty weighting sets σₖ² = Lₖ, so the gradient becomes ½∇log Lₖ. That is the same scale-invariant log-loss as FAMO's "SI" baseline and the same size-blindness as our `--critic-norm` ([Kendall et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.07115); [Liu et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03792)). Nash-MTL "is invariant to changes in loss scale". IMTL "was unstable on this RL benchmark and all of the runs crashed due to numerical overflow" ([Kurin et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.04122)). DWA is the exception: its weights lie in (0, 4) and multiply the raw losses, so error size survives. Kurin et al. and Xin et al. also predict our aggregator ranking. **Stabilised plain-sum training "performs on par with" specialised multi-task optimisers in Meta-World SAC, and multi-task optimisers "fail to improve the interference profile beyond what is achievable by simple static weighting"** ([Kurin et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.04122); [Xin et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11379)). PCGrad and CAGrad, which stay closest to the mean gradient, were our least bad critic aggregators (7.7 % and 12.3 % crashes). The more an aggregator rescales by inverse gradient size, the worse it crashed. Inside a shared critic, an aggregator can only redistribute one shared capacity among groups.

Three representation fixes are cheap and well supported. **Critic LayerNorm is among the strongest single interventions in SAC.** It is "more effective in reducing overestimation than techniques specifically designed" for it, and in TOPPO it was the largest single step on MT50 ([Nauman et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.00514); [Li et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.11473v3)). Placed before FAST_TANH, it caps hard saturation by construction. With gain near 1 and bias near 0, the 64 normalised pre-activations of each sample have mean square 1, so Markov's inequality allows at most 1/9 of units at |z| ≥ 3 (our derivation). Its learnable gain can undo this and should be monitored. Unit-ball normalisation of the penultimate features "decouples learning the scale of the values from the first layers of the network and moves it to the last linear layer" ([Hussing et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05996)). That stops the safety head from using trunk saturation to reach its large values, provided each head gets a fixed output multiplier so Adam is not left to grow the weights to the hundreds. Finally, because only the actor is deployed, the critic's activation can be swapped freely. A non-saturating activation removes dead units, although pre-activation shift can still cause other pathologies ([Lyle et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18762)). Bootstrapping fixes have only theoretical support: Lyle et al.'s TD-versus-Monte-Carlo contrast suggests n-step or Monte Carlo auxiliary targets for the dense heads. No paper uses per-head Polyak rates, per-head learning rates or stop-gradients between reward heads. Diversity regularisers such as EDAC's action-gradient cosine penalty or MED-RL push heads apart regardless of what the returns say, which risks wrong slopes ([An et al. 2021](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.01548)). Preference conditioning would add inputs to the deployed actor, and no paper measures whether it keeps heads distinct ([Abels et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1809.07803)).

## Re-pricing the crash beats re-weighting it

The intuitive fix, replacing the crash penalty with a survival bonus, changes nothing that matters. Our TD3 bootstraps at the 500-step limit, as Pardo et al. recommend for limits that exist only to help learning ([Pardo et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00378)). Under that partial-episode bootstrapping, a bonus b on every non-terminal step and a terminal penalty P = b/(1−γ) give returns that differ by the same constant on every trajectory. The greedy policy, ∇ₐQ and the set of states where crashing pays are therefore identical. L2F's +2 bonus with a terminal reward of 0 is a 200-point crash price ([L2F squared.h](https://github.com/arplaboratory/learning-to-fly/blob/master/include/learning_to_fly/simulator/parameters/reward_functions/squared.h#L62-L67)). RAPTOR's +1.5 bonus plus −100 is about 250 ([Eschmann et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.11481v2)). Our −400 equals a bonus of about 4. **The safety group's value spread is set by the crash price, not by where the price is booked, so a sign change leaves the 152-versus-3.2 gap intact.** Re-pricing to L2F's 200 would at most halve it. Crash prices in successful motor-level controllers cluster at 2 to 2.5 per step. RAPTOR's authors credit the move to an explicit termination penalty plus an action-rate term for letting them drop the curriculum, although this was not a controlled ablation ([Eschmann et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.11481v2)).

The reformulation that follows most directly from our measurements is to split the terminal penalty across groups. Give each group k a crash-step reward −Pₖ, with ΣₖPₖ = 400, and split the landing payout the same way. The scalar reward and variant E's utility are unchanged. If Pₖ is proportional to each group's typical discounted future cost Cₖ, every group prefers flying exactly when the scalar reward does, and no group gains from crashing. This is our derivation; no paper does it. It makes explicit, in the reward, what the total-value term does implicitly in the loss. Every head then carries crash information in its own units, so per-head normalisation or a scale-invariant aggregator can no longer switch crash avoidance off. The costs are higher inter-group value correlation (now real, so collapse must be measured on the dense parts only) and changed conflict statistics against the published split-critic numbers.

The principled alternative is a constraint. **With a crash cost of 1, the Lagrangian reward r − λc is exactly our reward with crash penalty λ, so variant E is a constrained MDP whose multiplier is frozen at 400.** RCPO or a PID Lagrangian would let λ track a target crash rate ([Tessler et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.11074); [Stooke et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2007.03964)). PID-Lagrangian adds damping against the oscillation that "leads to constraint-violating behavior". It also adds "invariance to the relative numerical scales of reward and cost" through the gradient-norm ratio β = ‖∇J‖/‖∇J_C‖, and it uses "separate value- and cost-value approximators" ([Stooke et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03964)). The cost critic then learns a discounted crash probability in [0, 1], as in SQRL's safety critic ([Srinivasan et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2010.14603)). The shared reward critic sees only dense groups about 5x apart, and the crash term never passes through a scale-invariant aggregation. CoMOGA already pairs scale-invariant objective constraints with absolute-unit safety constraints and a recovery step. Its CAGrad + Lagrangian ablation violated a constraint, which the authors attribute to "the concurrent update of policy and multipliers" ([Kim et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.00282v2)). There is a catch specific to us: with all-negative costs the reward objective itself favours crashing, so a pure constraint would bind all the time. The constraint should sit on top of a base penalty, or follow the split-penalty step, and its discount must match the quadrotor's horizon. SQRL's γ_safe = 0.7 is only about 30 ms at 100 Hz.

Shaping helps less than it seems. A potential-based term F = γΦ(s′) − Φ(s) preserves the optimal policy, but in episodic tasks only with zero terminal potential ([Ng et al. 1999](https://mlanthology.org/icml/1999/ng1999icml-policy/); [Grześ 2017](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/60614)). Its return telescopes to −Φ(s), so its true action-gradient is zero. A shaping term must never get its own head, because it would feed pure noise to the aggregator, which PGA would scale up. Inside the safety head, a crash-risk potential Φ_safe(s) = −P·p̂(s) has a useful property. It removes state-driven variance (doomed versus safe states) from the targets without changing the utility or ∇ₐQ, because shaping is equivalent to a change of initialisation ([Wiewiora 2003](https://arxiv.org/abs/1106.5267)). Whether this helps depends on how much of safety's spread is state-driven, which can be measured offline. Our progress candidate's crash step does not refund the remaining distance, so it is shaping plus an extra 2K·d crash penalty and must not be called potential-based. Bounded positive per-group rewards, such as SimpleFlight's terms "normalize[d] … to the range [0, 1]", would make every group's value lie in [0, 100] by construction and spread the crash price across all four streams ([Chen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.11764)). The cost is a new objective, so variant E would have to be retrained on the same reward. No quadrotor paper compares bounded and unbounded rewards head to head.

The utility-based MORL guide frames the remaining choices: fix the utility first, and treat safety-critical decisions as constraint-like or risk-sensitive rather than as one more weighted term ([Hayes et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.09568)). In practice this means three things. Separate the rare terminal event from the dense orientation and landing-speed limits, as the code's `REGROUPS` already does. Judge a group's scale by its decision relevance (advantages, ∇ₐQ), not by TD-target std. And keep the action-rate term, which was the best real-world smoothness term in SimpleFlight's ablation (0.016 / 0.028 / 0.051 m tracking error at slow, normal and fast speeds) ([Chen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.11764)). If the weight curriculum stays, our one-shot per-group scales are stale by up to the curriculum's factors (position 5 → 40, velocity and action 0.01 → 1). L2F also recalculates replay-buffer rewards at each change, and removing that recalculation cost real-flight successes in its ablation ([Eschmann et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13081)).

## Conclusion

The research reframes the problem. Neither "shared versus separate networks" nor "which normaliser" is the right question. The real question is how crash information reaches each per-group gradient. The split critic works partly because the total-value term couples its four networks and feeds the crash error into every cost head. Remove that term and the split critic crashes as often as the shared one. The shared critic fails because the same safety error that must reach every head also takes over the trunk: it saturates 39 % of its units and squeezes all four heads onto 11 feature directions, so the heads end up near-copies well above the true return correlation. This points to a contribution the paper does not yet claim: per-objective termination bias, and the observation that decomposing a reward with a terminal penalty creates groups that each gain from failing. It also explains why "accurate values" kept producing crashing policies. The critic answered the regression question it was given correctly, and three of its four answers favoured a crash.

For the paper this cuts two ways. The collapse claim is now reviewer-proof against the resolvent objection, thanks to the Monte Carlo reference. But "four independent critics" is no longer an accurate description of the method, and the total-value coupling must be reported. The most defensible way forward keeps variant E's utility fixed and changes only how the crash is represented: split across groups, isolated in its own network, or moved to a constraint critic. Every one of these is a structural argument a reviewer can accept as principled, whereas each normalisation we tried amounted to an implicit and harmful change of utility.
