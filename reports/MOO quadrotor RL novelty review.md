# Spectral aggregators and split critics claim open ground

Both papers aim at real gaps, but the gaps are narrower than the headlines suggest. **Paper 1 (PGA)** is novel in its orthogonal-NMF half: no gradient aggregator built on NMF or orthogonal NMF turned up anywhere. Its SVD half lands next to **Aligned-MTL (CVPR 2023)**, which already eigendecomposes the task Gramian and returns a whitened sum of every principal direction. Two more papers sit close by. **GDOD (CIKM 2022)** works from an orthogonal basis of the gradient span, and **FedLD (MICCAI 2024)** already uses the phrase "principal gradient" for eigenvector-based aggregation in federated learning. SVD-PGA therefore has to argue novelty on top-k truncation, σ-weighting and its sign rule, not on "using SVD". **Paper 2** has the stronger novelty position. Within the searches run, nobody has published TD3 with separate per-objective critics whose actor gradients are combined by conflict-aware aggregators for motor-level quadrotor control. Nobody has reported per-head value collapse in a shared multi-head critic. Nobody has measured how a shared critic hides gradient conflict that separate critics reveal. Its weak point is contribution 3. Two NeurIPS 2022 papers show that well-tuned scalarization matches most multi-task optimizers. The crash-rate gains do not survive multiple-comparison correction, and SVD-PGA only roughly matches hand-tuned TD3. The closest competitors reviewers will raise are Xing et al. (per-task critics on a real quadrotor), GCR-PPO (per-reward-component conflict projection, which measured *low* conflict on a quadcopter), SAC-D (a multi-head critic that needed CAGrad), CoMOGA and MOAC (per-objective critics with aggregation in the actor), and MultiCriticAL. All of these novelty findings rest on absence of evidence from bounded searches. No Google Scholar "cited by" sweep was done, so they are moderate-to-high confidence, not proof.

## Every learned low-level quadrotor controller still optimizes one hand-weighted scalar

The lineage Paper 2 builds on runs from Hwangbo et al.'s 2017 RA-L paper, the first motor-command RL policy flown on a real quadrotor, which recovered from being thrown upside-down at 5 m/s ([arXiv 1707.05110](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.05110)). It continues through Molchanov et al.'s sim-to-multi-real transfer of state-to-motor policies at IROS 2019 ([arXiv 1903.04628](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.04628)), Lambert et al.'s model-based Crazyflie hover in RA-L 2019 ([PDF](https://drl.ece.utah.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/155/2021/07/lambert-ral2019.pdf)) and Gronauer et al.'s IROS 2022 simulation-optimization work ([arXiv 2201.01369](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.01369)). The direct ancestor is **Eschmann, Albani and Loianno, "Learning to Fly in Seconds" (RA-L 2024)**. It uses an off-policy asymmetric actor-critic whose critic sees 28 dimensions of privileged state, with direct RPM outputs, and its Crazyflie policy transfers after **18 s of training on a laptop** ([arXiv 2311.13081](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13081); [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.13081)). The same group's **RAPTOR** (arXiv 2025, revised 2026) distills 1,000 teacher policies into a 2,084-parameter recurrent student that adapts zero-shot on 10 real quadrotors from 32 g to 2.4 kg ([arXiv 2509.11481](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.11481)). Ferede et al. (ICRA 2024) show that end-to-end motor commands beat a CTBR+INDI pipeline by 0.17 s per lap in the real world ([arXiv 2311.16948](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.16948v1)). On the CTBR side, **SimpleFlight (RA-L 2025)** runs PPO on a Crazyflie 2.1 and reaches 0.016 / 0.028 / 0.051 m figure-eight error at slow, normal and fast speeds ([HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.11764v4)). Swift (Nature 2023) is the champion-level racing reference ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06419-4)).

Each of these papers settles the precision, effort, smoothness and safety trade-off by hand. Eschmann's reward is a weighted sum of position, attitude, velocity, body-rate and action penalties plus a survival bonus. A curriculum multiplies the penalty weights every 100k steps, which amounts to **a hand-scheduled change of scalarization weights** ([HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.13081)). SimpleFlight picked its smoothness weight **λ = 0.4 from real-world sweeps**. Its authors warn that a larger λ restricts agility and a smaller one destabilizes flight ([HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.11764v4)). CAPS (ICRA 2021) adds smoothness as a policy regularizer and reports about 80% lower power on a real quadrotor ([arXiv 2012.06644](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.06644v2)). This is Paper 2's best motivation: the community openly acknowledges the trade-off, tunes it by hand, and never reports it as a front.

Three papers come closest to decomposing the objective, and none does what Paper 2 does. **Xing, Geles, Song, Aljalbout and Scaramuzza, "Multi-Task RL for Quadrotors" (RA-L, accepted Nov 2024)** uses one shared CTBR actor and **"a separate critic function … for each task"**, trained with PPO and flown on a real Agilicious quadrotor. Its critics are per *task* (stabilization, velocity tracking, racing as separate MDPs), and the actor simply maximizes the average return. The paper cites CAGrad for "conflicting objectives" but uses no conflict resolution ([HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.12442)). **Liu et al. (arXiv 2503.08259, 2025)** condition a graph-policy SAC on a 7-feature reward-weight vector, which is effectively preference-conditioned MORL. It flies an F450 at the attitude level, but it uses a single critic and handles conflict structurally through graph edges, without measuring it ([HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.08259v1)). **Yu and Lee (arXiv 2311.06144)** split control into translation and yaw agents with separate rewards. That is a multi-agent axis decomposition, tested sim-to-sim ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.06144)). Targeted searches for multi-objective, Pareto, preference-conditioned or Lagrangian treatment at the RPM or CTBR level found nothing else. Brushless Crazyflie RL is almost empty: the one hit is a Cal Poly thesis with an Isaac Lab hover policy at 0.081 m mean error over 28 flights ([thesis](https://digitalcommons.calpoly.edu/theses/3375/)).

The evaluation protocol Paper 2 should reuse is also settled. Eschmann flies a figure-eight at T = 15 / 5.5 / 3.5 s under Vicon with 10 seeds and reports RMSE. It also reports **0.24 m at the fast setting, against 0.36 m for a geometric controller and 0.52 m for Molchanov** ([HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.13081)). No paper in this lineage reports effort or smoothness metrics as carefully as tracking error, except CAPS for power. Reporting ‖Δu‖, motor saturation and crash rate next to RMSE is an evaluation niche Paper 2 can own.

## Multi-objective UAV RL lives at the mission level, far above the motors

The UAV MORL literature is large but orthogonal to Paper 2. It mostly comes from the wireless community and covers coverage, throughput, age of information and energy in simulation. Examples are DRL-EC3 in IEEE JSAC 2018, which scalarizes coverage, fairness, energy and connectivity ([DBLP](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/journals/jsac/LiuCTXP18.html)), evolutionary multi-objective PPO for a UAV edge server ([arXiv 2501.06410](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.06410)), evolutionary MORL for swarms ([arXiv 2502.05824](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.05824v1)) and attention-based MORL for IoT data collection ([arXiv 2601.14092](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.14092v1)). Formation-control work does use vector rewards: a 4-D reward (progress, energy, coherence, safety) with vector critics and a Pareto archive appears in MDPI Drones 2025 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/drones9120845)), and a multi-UAV formation paper scalarizes a vector reward ([arXiv 2410.18495](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.18495)). The low-level dynamics are abstracted away in all of them, and **no gradient-aggregation method (MGDA, PCGrad, etc.) appeared in any UAV paper found**.

The only "MORL plus Crazyflie" hit is **Javeed, "Model-assisted RL of a Quadrotor" (arXiv 2311.06914, 2023)**. It is a single-author preprint with high-level waypoint actions tracked by a PID. It formulates run-time weight switching but runs **no multi-objective experiments**, and its hardware flights are unconfirmed ([HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.06914)). It does not pre-empt Paper 2, but it should be cited to close off the obvious reviewer search. Safe RL on the Crazyflie mostly means safety filters around position-level policies: Pizarro Bejarano et al. (RA-L 2025) on a real Crazyflie 2.0 ([PDF](https://www.dynsyslab.org/wp-content/papercite-data/pdf/pizarro-ral25.pdf)), Du et al.'s learned control Lyapunov barrier function ([arXiv 2305.09793](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.09793)), SafeSwarm ([arXiv 2501.07566](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.07566v1)) and safe-control-gym ([arXiv 2109.06325](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.06325)). The nearest Lagrangian work is a 2026 PPO-Lagrangian method on a reduced-order model in which attitude is a reference for an inner loop ([arXiv 2604.10429](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.10429)). Since Paper 2 treats safety as a separate objective, it should explain why it did not cast safety as a constraint. A constrained formulation is the obvious alternative a reviewer will name. The best non-drone analogue is **PROMO (arXiv 2610.01260)**, preference-conditioned MORL on a real Unitree Go2. Preference changes alone cut specific energy by up to 30.4% and position error by 38.7% ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01260)). It shows what a hardware multi-objective result is expected to look like.

## Per-objective critics with actor-gradient aggregation exist, but not for TD3 on a quadrotor

The method side of Paper 2 has three verified close competitors. **GCR-PPO (Munn, Tidd, Böhm, Gallagher, Howard; arXiv Sept 2025, no venue listed)** is the most dangerous. It uses PPO with a vector-valued critic, one head per reward component, and component-wise GAE. Gradients are projected in PCGrad style by priority, so a regularizer gradient is projected off a task gradient when they conflict. It reports **+9.5% over massively parallel PPO** across IsaacLab tasks ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.14816)). It also measured conflict on the IsaacLab Quadcopter and found it **the lowest of all 13 tasks (0.15 conflicting pairs on average), with only a +1.09% gain**. The authors attribute the small gain to low conflict, and conflict correlates with improvement at Spearman ρ = 0.736 across tasks ([HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.14816)). This bears directly on Paper 2's contribution 2, which reports precision-vs-efficiency conflict in 82% of updates. Paper 2 must reconcile the two findings. The likely explanations: the IsaacLab Quadcopter is a simple goal-reaching task with few reward terms (the paper does not list them), and the method is on-policy with a shared value. Paper 2 uses motor-level control with four reward groups and separate Q-networks. GCR-PPO uses no real robot and does not run PCGrad, CAGrad or CoMOGA as baselines ([HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.14816)).

**CoMOGA (Kim, Hong, Park, Oh; ICLR 2025)** is the off-policy precedent. It uses SAC-style reparameterized gradients, separate reward critics per objective and cost critics per constraint, and a QP aggregation inside a trust region. It was tested on legged robots, Safety-Gymnasium and MO-MuJoCo, in simulation only. Its CAGrad+Lagrangian ablation did worse than CoMOGA ([HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.00282v2); [ICLR PDF](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/59ddfff7979b43f54690fa986c0e5138-Paper-Conference.pdf)). **MOAC (Zhou et al., ICML 2024)** is the theoretical precedent. It uses per-objective linear critics on a shared feature map and an MGDA min-norm actor with momentum on the weights, with convergence bounds that do not depend on the number of objectives. Its experiments are small: MO-Gymnasium tasks and a recommender log ([HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.03082)). In multi-task RL, PCGrad, CAGrad and Nash-MTL have been tested on Meta-World MT10 with SAC. The reported success ladder is **MT-SAC 0.49, PCGrad 0.72, CAGrad 0.83, Nash-MTL 0.91, one SAC per task 0.90** ([CAGrad](https://arxiv.org/html/2110.14048); [Nash-MTL](https://arxiv.org/html/2202.01017); [PCGrad](https://arxiv.org/html/2001.06782)). Those gradients are per-task gradients through shared, task-conditioned networks, not gradients from per-reward critics. The established MORL frameworks in MORL-Baselines (Envelope Q, PGMORL, CAPQL, PD-MORL, GPI-PD, PCN) scalarize a vector or preference-conditioned Q and do not apply per-objective gradient surgery ([GitHub](https://github.com/LucasAlegre/morl-baselines)).

Paper 2's method niche is therefore **off-policy TD3 with one separate Q-network per reward group, whose deterministic actor gradients ∇_a Q_k · ∇_θ π are combined by eight aggregators, at the motor level, aimed at real hardware**. No verified MO-TD3 or MO-DDPG paper of this kind surfaced. This is the strongest single novelty claim of the project, provided it is framed as an extension of CoMOGA and MOAC (off-policy, per-objective critics) and of GCR-PPO (reward-component conflict), not as a first in kind.

## A shared multi-head critic's failure modes are known in outline but never measured

Decomposed critics are an old idea. **HRA (van Seijen et al., NeurIPS 2017)** sums per-component Q heads and notes that heads "can share multiple lower-level layers" ([arXiv 1706.04208](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04208v2)). **SAC-D (MacGlashan et al., NeurIPS 2022)** gives each reward component a Q head and sums them. It is the closest prior to contribution 1: the authors found that naive multi-head SAC-D **"significantly underperforms SAC"**. On Ant, termination-induced Q errors caused "catastrophic gradient conflicts", which they fixed by treating each component as a CAGrad task ([NeurIPS PDF](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/file/4eb2c0adafbe71269f3a772c130f9e53-Paper-Conference.pdf)). **MultiCriticAL (ICLR 2022)** contrasts separate critic networks with one multi-head critic. It argues that separate networks help "when sharing is likely to lead to undesirable interference" and reports up to 56% gains ([OpenReview](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=rJvY_5OzoI); [poster](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2022/poster/6568)). SLIM motivates per-component critics the same way ([arXiv 2402.00823](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.00823)). Reward-adaptive bipedal RL ([arXiv 2107.01908](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.01908)), HARC in 2026 robotics ([arXiv 2608.09762](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09762)) and a 2026 ICML-workshop LLM critic ([listing](https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/83684)) all use shared-trunk heads and present them as beneficial. On normalization, PopArt and multi-task PopArt rescale each head so that "all tasks have a similar impact" ([arXiv 1809.04474](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1809.04474)), and MOPPO applies per-objective PopArt ([arXiv 2407.16807](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.16807)).

Against this background, both halves of contribution 1 look unreported. First, no source analyzes how summing K heads on a shared trunk multiplies the effective step of the total value. The 2.3x figure for K = 4 fits the mechanism in which every head's TD gradient moves the shared trunk, and that reasoning is the research team's own; no paper states it. HRA is internally inconsistent on whether its aggregator sums or averages heads ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1706.04208)), and VDN passes gradients through a summation without any step-size analysis ([arXiv 1706.05296](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.05296)). Second, **no paper reports per-head critic values collapsing into copies**. The "collapse" in D3PO is policy behaviour collapsing across preferences ([HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.07764v1)), and in MOPPO it is entropy collapse ([arXiv 2407.16807](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.16807)). D3PO and SAC-D argue in general terms that early scalarization hides interference. SAC-D notes that per-component optimization introduces conflicts even though the composite Q is the same as SAC's ([NeurIPS PDF](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/file/4eb2c0adafbe71269f3a772c130f9e53-Paper-Conference.pdf)). But **no paper compares a shared-trunk multi-head critic with separate critic networks under a fixed actor update rule and measures gradient cosine**. The nearest attempt, a 2026 dual-vs-unified humanoid critic study, compares separate critics with a *scalar* critic, uses single unseeded runs, and its author admits the design "cannot separate the critic from the update rule" ([HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.11891)). Paper 2's controlled comparison would fill exactly that gap.

The weakness is that a reviewer can recast contribution 1 as a known pathology with a known fix. On this view, the 2.3x step is just a learning-rate mismatch, and per-head PopArt or a tuned critic learning rate should cure it without separate networks. The paper needs to show that a shared critic with per-head PopArt and a matched learning rate still collapses or still hides conflict. Without that control, contribution 1 reads as a tuning artifact rather than an architectural finding. The broader value-network collapse literature should also be cited, so the paper can show that head-to-head similarity is a different phenomenon: implicit under-parameterization ([arXiv 2010.14498](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14498)) and capacity loss ([arXiv 2204.09560](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.09560)), both noted from memory in the research.

## SVD-PGA must beat Aligned-MTL on its own axis; OS-NMF-PGA has no direct precedent

The aggregator field holds about 25 methods, and **TorchJD ships 23 of them**, including UPGrad, DualProj, Aligned-MTL, CAGrad, ConFIG, MGDA, Nash-MTL, PCGrad, FairGrad and PCD ([TorchJD docs](https://torchjd.org/stable/docs/aggregation/)). LibMTL is the other reference implementation ([LibMTL](https://libmtl.readthedocs.io/en/latest/py-modindex.html)). Recent work concentrates on dual-cone feasibility and convergence theory rather than spectral structure. Examples are UPGrad/DualProj from "Jacobian Descent", listed as accepted at NeurIPS 2026 ([arXiv 2406.16232](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16232)), Priority-Constrained Descent (TMLR 2026) ([OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=HT01yGHLEt)), Capped MGDA with a unified alignment framework (2026) ([arXiv 2605.30452](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30452)), ConFIG at ICLR 2025 ([proceedings](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/94e85561a342de88b559b72c9b29f638-Abstract-Conference.html)) and GradOPS at WSDM 2025 ([arXiv 2503.03438](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.03438)). The spectral corner is uncrowded, but it has one strong occupant.

**Aligned-MTL (Senushkin, Patakin, Kuznetsov, Konushin; CVPR 2023)** eigendecomposes M = GᵀG and sets every singular value to σ_min. Its update is ĝ = σ_min U Vᵀ w, the polar factor of G. It converges to a Pareto-stationary point that is optimal for preset weights w, and it was evaluated on NYUv2, CityScapes and Meta-World MT10 with SAC ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.19000); [CVPR](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2023/html/Senushkin_Independent_Component_Alignment_for_Multi-Task_Learning_CVPR_2023_paper.html)). Algebraically, Aligned-MTL is already σ_min Σ_i (v_iᵀw) u_i, a weighted sum of *all* left principal directions with equal magnitudes. The research team derived what follows from this (it is not a published result). An SVD aggregator that keeps σ_i weighting and all k = r directions reduces to the plain weighted mean G w. One that whitens reduces to Aligned-MTL. Top-k truncation is a third pole. **SVD-PGA's novelty therefore lives entirely in truncation, σ-weighting and its orientation rule**, and the paper should contain an ablation that recovers both the mean and Aligned-MTL as special cases. Two more works narrow the claim. GDOD (Dong et al., CIKM 2022) updates along task-shared components taken from an orthogonal basis of span(G) ([arXiv 2301.13465](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.13465)). FedLD (Zeng et al., MICCAI 2024) proposes "a principal gradient-based server aggregation strategy" using eigenvectors of GᵀG with sign calibration against a reference gradient ([arXiv 2408.12300](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.12300)). FedLD pre-empts the name "principal gradient" and the sign-calibration idea, so both must be cited.

**OS-NMF-PGA is the more novel half.** Targeted searches found no NMF- or orthogonal-NMF-based gradient aggregator; the only hits were deep-NMF clustering and NMF for ranking ([arXiv 1711.07437](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.07437)). Gradient entries are signed, so the method must factorize either a semi-NMF of G or the non-negative part of the Gramian. A non-negative coefficient matrix makes each factor a conic combination of task gradients, which gives a clean "weighted" property and removes SVD's sign ambiguity. Task-grouping work that clusters gradient-affinity matrices was not surveyed and is the likely nearest neighbour.

Three theoretical gaps will draw reviewer fire. Raw SVD is **not scale-invariant**, because a large-norm objective dominates u_1. That is the problem Aligned-MTL's whitening, Nash-MTL's bargaining rule and IMTL-G's/ConFIG's unit-gradient normalization each address. Top-k directions are **not guaranteed non-conflicting** when gradients conflict. Perron–Frobenius guarantees a non-negative v_1 only when all pairwise cosines are non-negative, so a dual-cone projection in the UPGrad style would be needed for a provable non-conflict property. The 2026 unified framework then supplies a ready-made convergence argument ([arXiv 2605.30452](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30452)). Finally, the empirical bar is high. Xin et al. (NeurIPS 2022) found that multi-task optimizers gave no gains over properly weighted scalarization at scale ([arXiv 2209.11379](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11379)). Kurin et al. (NeurIPS 2022) showed that the regularized plain sum matches or beats specialized optimizers in both supervised learning and RL ([arXiv 2201.04122](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.04122)). Random loss weighting is also competitive ([arXiv 2111.10603](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.10603)). The drone application inherits that bar. On the quadrotor, SVD-PGA "roughly matches" hand-tuned TD3. That shows the method works there, not that it is superior, and Paper 1 should present it as a working application, not as a win.

## Contribution-by-contribution verdict

| Claim | Novelty | Closest prior | What undercuts it | What would secure it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P1: SVD-PGA method | **Moderate.** It differentiates only on truncation, σ-weighting and orientation | Aligned-MTL; GDOD; FedLD | Aligned-MTL is already an SVD sum of all principal directions; FedLD owns the term "principal gradient"; no scale invariance or non-conflict guarantee | Ablation recovering mean and Aligned-MTL; a scale-normalized variant; a dual-cone-projected variant with a convergence proof |
| P1: OS-NMF-PGA method | **High.** No precedent found | Task-grouping by gradient affinity (not surveyed) | Signed gradients make plain NMF ill-posed; factorization cost per step | A clear semi-/symmetric-NMF formulation; a conic-hull property statement |
| P1: quadrotor application | **High as an application, weak as evidence** | GCR-PPO quadcopter; Xing et al. | Only "roughly matches" hand-tuned TD3; scalarization critiques | Standard MTL benchmarks (NYUv2, CityScapes, QM9, MT10) in addition to the drone |
| P2-1: 2.3x step / 1/K collapse | **High.** Head collapse and summed-head step size are unreported | SAC-D; HRA; PopArt; MultiCriticAL | Can be read as a learning-rate or normalization artifact | Shared critic with per-head PopArt and a matched learning rate as a control |
| P2-2: separate critics reveal 68-82% conflict | **High.** There is no controlled shared-vs-separate gradient-cosine measurement in the literature | GCR-PPO (conflict metric); dual-vs-unified humanoid; MultiCriticAL | GCR-PPO measured the *lowest* conflict on a quadcopter | Same conflict metric as GCR-PPO (fraction of pairs at >90°); explanation of the difference |
| P2-3: lower crash rates, SVD-PGA ≈ TD3, faster and smoother | **Low to moderate.** The off-policy TD3 + 8-aggregator setup is new; the outcome is weak | CoMOGA; MOAC; Xin et al.; Kurin et al. | Not significant after correction; no MOO arm beats TD3; reweighting may just shift implicit weights | A tuned linear-scalarization sweep (a Pareto front from weights) as the baseline; more seeds; hardware flights |

The faster-waypoint and smoother-motor results need care. Reweighting aggregators change the effective weights on the four groups, so a tuned scalar reward with shifted weights might reproduce them. The Xin/Kurin critique predicts exactly this. The defensible framing is that aggregators find a different operating point *without a weight search*. This is the Eschmann-curriculum and SimpleFlight-λ motivation made concrete, rather than a claim that they dominate.

## Conclusion

The two papers are stronger together than apart, but their honest headlines differ from the planned ones. Paper 1's contribution is a spectral and conic family that places the mean, Aligned-MTL and top-k principal directions on one axis, plus the first NMF-based aggregator. The quadrotor is a demanding application, not proof of superiority, and the paper earns acceptance through the ablation that recovers Aligned-MTL and through standard MTL benchmarks. Paper 2's most defensible contribution is diagnostic: shared multi-head critics, the default in decomposed RL since HRA, can manufacture apparent agreement between objectives. Separate critics show that low-level flight objectives conflict heavily, in direct tension with GCR-PPO's low-conflict quadcopter. That reframes the aggregator null result. It suggests earlier "MTL optimizers don't help" findings in RL may partly reflect critics that hid the conflict, a testable hypothesis no one has published.

The practical priority is to secure contributions 1 and 2 with controls (per-head PopArt, matched learning rates, the GCR-PPO conflict metric). Contribution 3 should be presented as matching hand-tuned performance without a weight search, which the scalarization critiques cannot dismiss, rather than as a crash-rate win they will. Hardware flights on the Crazyflie 2.1 Brushless would make Paper 2 the first real-robot result in a field where every per-objective-critic method so far, including GCR-PPO and CoMOGA, has stayed in simulation.
